# Research: "Why is the Colosseum covered in holes?"

Source research for a 30-second film. Checked 16 November 2026. Working answer: the travertine blocks were held together by metal (iron) clamps set in lead; after the building fell out of use, people dug the metal out, leaving the holes. Verdicts: verified (read in a scholarly or primary source, or two independent sources), uncertain, false.

**Reading limits, stated first.** I could not read any of the standard modern books. Claridge (*Rome: An Oxford Archaeological Guide*), Hopkins & Beard (*The Colosseum*), Lancaster (*Concrete Vaulted Construction*, the chapter on clamps is behind a paywall), Rea, Cozzo (1971), Lugli (1957, 1971), Gabucci (ed., *Il Colosseo*, Electa 1999) and Coarelli are either lending-only on archive.org (401 on the text) or not online. ## 1. The clamps: material, lead, shape, size, mortar

| Claim | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| The travertine blocks were joined by metal clamps/pins | **verified** (three independent descriptions, none of them a book I could read) | Art of Making, PR203_01_01: "The pock-marked facade of the structure in its current state is a result of the removal of iron clamps which were originally used to hold the blocks of travertine together." Manieri Elia (thesis 2003, pp. 107-120): "metal pins were placed in appropriate slots cut into the faces of the blocks" (Italian: "perni metallici in appositi alloggiamenti, scalpellati nelle facce dei conci"). the-colosseum.net, building page: "The travertine blocks were all connected to each other by iron clamps". Wikipedia, "Colosseum", cites Claridge for "iron clamps which held the stonework together". |
| The metal was **iron** | **verified as the standard statement; no analysis found** | "Iron" in Art of Making (PR203_01_01 and tools/18: "Iron clamps were usually used but tended to be wrapped in lead to prevent them rusting"), the-colosseum.net and Wikipedia. Other entries and Manieri Elia say only "metal". I found **no metallurgical analysis** of a surviving clamp or of the residue in a socket. A few popular pages say bronze. Suaresius's quoted authority "Ciaconius" (Alfonso Chacón, d. 1599) speaks of bronze: `aeneis clavis` is my reading of the OCR "antis clauis" (p. 5), so even the 16th-century writers were guessing. Safe: "metal clamps" or "iron clamps" with no further claim. |
| The clamps were **set in lead** | **verified (one scholarly source)** | Manieri Elia, English translation: "the positioning completed, molten lead was poured into special channels carved into the lower block that conveyed the lead to the pins already positioned inside, which by this means were fixed effectively in place" (Italian: "si colava piombo fuso in apposite canalette scalpellate nel blocco inferiore"). Art of Making tools/18 gives the general Roman practice ("set in molten lead to hold them in place") without naming the Colosseum. I found no source that has seen lead in a Colosseum socket. Safe: "set in molten lead" attributed loosely, or just "metal clamps". |
| Clamps and **dowels** (pins) | **uncertain** | Manieri Elia calls the fixings "pins" (perni) and the same text uses "clamps" (grappe) for them; he does not distinguish the two types. Art of Making PR203_01_14 records separate "pin holes" at an inscription repair, which is a different, later feature. Do not say "dowels". |
| **Shape** (dovetail, C, pi) and **size** of the clamps | **uncertain; no source found** | Popular pages say "C-shaped and dovetail clamps" without citation. Lancaster's chapter summary says "the earliest datable use of clamps in Rome are the iron pi clamps used to attach the travertine facing" of the Temple of Castor (that is Rome in general, not the Colosseum). Do not give a shape or a size. |
| **No mortar** between the blocks | **uncertain; disputed** | Wikipedia (citing Claridge): "set without mortar". But Manieri Elia, citing Lugli 1957, p. 243: "Before laying every single stone in position, the surface of the block underneath was smeared with a thin layer of liquid lime mortar: this fluid layer aided the sliding of the masses one against the other and, at the same time, rendered the parts in contact more cohesive". So: no bedding mortar in the ordinary sense, but a thin lime grout layer is reported. Safe: "stacked without a mortar bed, tied with metal", or leave mortar out. Do not write "no mortar at all". |
| Lifting holes also exist in the blocks | **verified (one source)** | Manieri Elia: "in almost all the blocks there are conspicuous holes to allow them to be hooked up" (Italian: "in quasi tutti sono presenti cospicui fori praticati al fine di consentirne l'aggancio"). These are original, not robbing holes. See section 5. |
| The clamps mattered structurally | **uncertain** | Manieri Elia argues they served against shear in earthquakes. the-colosseum.net / popular claims that the wall "was flexible" or that removal caused the 1349 collapse are not supported by anything I read. Do not say the holes made the building fall. |

## 2. "300 tons of iron"

**Where the figure appears, in order of how scholarly the page is:**

| Where | Wording | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Art of Making, PR203_01_01 (King's College London, Leverhulme) | "Approximately 300 tonnes of metal, it has been estimated, was used in the construction of the Colosseum." | No author, no source given. The figure is for metal **used in building**, not for what was later removed. "Metal", not "iron". |
| the-colosseum.net, building page | "It has been calculated that - only for these clamps - the builders used 300 tons of metal." | The page's stated main source is G. Cozzo, *Il Colosseo* (Palombi, 1971), but the sentence is not attributed to him, and I could not read the book. |
| the-colosseum.net, "Numbers" page | "300 tons of metal were used for the iron clamps that connected the limestone blocks together." Same page: "Over 100.000 cubic metres of travertine stone were used (45.000 for the external wall only)". | Says "limestone" for travertine. |
| English Wikipedia, "Colosseum" | "The outer wall is estimated to have required over 100,000 cubic metres of travertine stone which were set without mortar; they were held together by 300 tons of iron clamps." | The footnote is Claridge (1998), "Rome: An Oxford Archaeological Guide", pp. 276-282, with no page for this sentence. **Not read by me.** Wikipedia's attribution does not show that Claridge wrote it. |
| Popular pages (ticket sites, blogs) | "300 tons" or "200-300 tons"; "iron and bronze" in one | Cite nothing. |

**Who estimated it, and in which publication: not established.** Candidate origins are Cozzo (1971) and Claridge (1998), both unread, and neither is confirmed. I found no calculation (number of blocks, joints, clamp size, density). Every source says "estimated" or "calculated"; none says by whom. It is not a measurement: no one could have weighed what was removed.

**Verdict:** a modern estimate, **uncertain**; firm enough to say "an estimate", not firm enough to give as a fact. Three details to keep straight: it is metal **used**, not metal **recovered**; the sources say "metal" or "iron clamps" interchangeably; Wikipedia attaches it to the outer wall, the-colosseum.net to the whole building.

**Safest spoken phrasing:** "Estimates put the metal in the walls at around 300 tons." (not "300 tons were stolen", not "exactly", not "of iron" unless the picture only shows iron). If the film wants no risk: "hundreds of tons".

## 3. Who removed the clamps, and when

| Claim | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| The clamps were removed after the building ceased to function as an amphitheatre ("post-Roman") | **verified (consistent across the sources I read)** | Art of Making PR203_01_14: "The holes visible on this surface were caused by the removal of metal clamps in the post-Roman period." PR203_01_01: "In the post-Roman period these clamps were more valuable when melted down as raw materials than they were holding these blocks in place." Manieri Elia: "the traces of the laborious operation carried out during the Middle Ages to retrieve the metal materials which were particularly sought-after in that period." the-colosseum.net: "extracted in medieval times". |
| Removal happened in the **Gothic War, 535-553** | **uncertain** (one site, hedged) | the-colosseum.net, "Middle Ages": "it was probably during the Gothic War that most of the metal grips holding the travertine blocks together were systematically removed." It adds that spoliation "started during the reign of Theodoric ... and it was systematic" and that "We have very few literary or epigraphic sources for the centuries VI-IX". No excavation, document or analysis is cited for the clamps. |
| Removal happened in a **specific century** | **false as a claim of knowledge** | Sources give "post-Roman", "the Middle Ages", "probably the Gothic War". Low-grade pages say 6th century or 14th-18th centuries with no support. I found no scholarly source that dates the clamp removal, and none that says scholars agree. |
| **Barbarians** did it | **uncertain at best; do not say** | This is the older popular explanation (section 4). A source I read that mentions the Goths ties the clamp removal to wartime scarcity of metal, not to a deliberate demolition attempt. Suaresius already called the "Goths tried to pull it down" story something done by those "who wish to be thought more learned" and gave reasons to doubt it (section 4). |
| The Parco's own excavation dates robbing | **verified, but for the floor, not the clamps** | https://colosseo.it/evento/tutela-piazza-colosseo-scavo-settore-sud/ (south sector, dig diary 2022-23): travertine removal "databile presumibilmente ad età bassomedievale (XI-XII secolo)" (15 Nov 2022); "spoliazione dei blocchi della pavimentazione in travertino che si può far risalire al XII-XIII secolo" (Feb 2023); collapse of two outer corridors from "demolizioni e cause naturali (terremoti) già a partire dal VI-VII secolo". This shows the stone was being robbed in the 11th-13th centuries; it does not date the clamps. |
| The 1349 earthquake collapsed the south side | **verified** (standard) | Wikipedia: "Severe damage ... by the great earthquake in 1349, causing the outer south side ... to collapse"; Cerone, Croci, Viskovic (2000) p. 2 of the PDF: "A new destructive earthquake ... took place in 1349 as testified by Petrarca." The Parco's diary hopes a horse skeleton found under the collapse confirms it (pending radiocarbon). Do not link it to the clamps. |

**Do scholars disagree?** I can only say the sources I could read disagree in emphasis ("post-Roman", "Middle Ages", "Gothic War, probably") and none is a dated excavation result. I could not read Rea, Claridge or Hopkins & Beard, who may say more.

**Safest spoken phrasing:** "Later, in the centuries after Rome fell, people dug the metal out." Do not say "barbarians", "Goths", "in the Middle Ages" as a precise date, or any century.

## 4. Older rival explanations and who proposed them

**Suaresius exists and I read it.** Joseph-Marie Suarès (Suaresius, bishop of Vaison), *Diatriba de foraminibus lapidum in priscis aedificiis*, dedicated to Cardinal Carlo Barberini. Two archive.org records: 1651 and Lyon 1652 (Guillaume Barbier). It is about the holes in the Colosseum's travertine (and other ancient buildings) and it is a **menu of rejected and half-accepted explanations, not a verdict for the clamp-robbing theory**.

| Explanation | Who | Evidence in Suaresius |
|---|---|---|
| Earthquake at Christ's death split the stones | "the unlearned crowd" (vulgus) | p. 2: "Et imperitum quidem vulgus petras hasce scissas eo ex terraemotu, quo concussa tellus tremuit universa Christo Domino nostro animam efflante, fabulatur. Id autem commentum plane ridiculum est." ("The ignorant crowd tells that these stones were split by the earthquake that shook the whole earth when our Lord gave up his soul. That invention is plainly ridiculous.") He answers that the buildings were not yet built, and that the holes are cut, not cracks: "manu & arte sunt facta foramina". |
| Goths (Totila's) tried to destroy the city | "those who wish to be thought more learned" | p. 2: "Qui eruditiores haberi volunt, foramina illa Gotthis Urbem diruere ... adscribunt." He rejects it with the Procopius story of Totila being dissuaded by Belisarius. |
| Beams for houses (columbaria, "dove-cote" holes) | unnamed ("non desunt qui...") | p. 3, citing Vitruvius. |
| Barricade posts (repagula) | unnamed | p. 3. |
| **Poles for market stalls** | Donatus (cited; probably Alessandro Donati, *Roma vetus ac recens*, 1639; I did not check this), reporting Bernardino Stefonio | p. 4: "Inferiores pilae, & arcus extima facie crebris foraminibus deformati sunt; id docti, indoctique referunt ad feritatem Barbarorum ... Equidem probabilius semper duxi, idque de Bernardino Stephonio ... audivi, hoc peractum opera artificum & institorum ... cum ibi nundinas agere ... consuessent ..., ut perticas immitterent sustinendis tabernarum, merciumque tegminibus" (stallholders drove tools into the stone to set poles for the stall awnings). The OCR does not make clear where Donatus's quotation ends. The same page quotes Donatus saying that "learned and unlearned" alike blame "the savagery of the Barbarians", so the barbarian story was current by 1639. Suaresius adds that in 1192 Cencius lists "Bandonarii Colossei" (craftsmen) working at the Colosseum (pp. 4-5). |
| Metal robbing of "iron, bronze and lead that bound the stones" | Ciaconius (Chacón) says holes in vaults and at stone joints are made "non minus a Romanis quam a Barbaris evellendi aeris causa" (p. 5); Suaresius himself lists those who "ferrum, aes, plumbum, quibus lapides devincti erant, diripuerunt, sive Graecis, sive Romanis, sive Gotthis, quos avaritia occaecavit" (p. 9) | pp. 5-7 add ancient parallels for stone joined with iron and lead (Thucydides on the Piraeus walls, Josephus, Statius' "gomphi", Theodahad's order about iron hooks), which is the theory of the clamps already in full form in 1651. He attributes the removal to avarice of "Greeks, Romans or Goths" (Byzantine, Roman or Gothic scavengers), not to a single event. |
| Rings to tie animals, ships, or hang pulleys for treasure-digging | unnamed | p. 7. |
| Holes made when the stones were built in (for lifting tongs, *forfices*) | citing Vitruvius | p. 9. |
| Holes for perfume pipes (*fistulae*) and statue fixings | his own addition | pp. 9-10. |
| His conclusion | | p. 9: "Quod si mihi ius esset in Antiquariorum Senatu ferendi suffragii, constarem e sex istis sententiis unam" (my reading: "if I had a vote in the senate of antiquarians I would construct one opinion out of these six"). He does not choose the clamp theory alone. |

**Other proposers.** Italian Wikipedia says Carlo Fea (excavations 1811-12) read the holes as "sistema per rimuovere le grappe metalliche"; it cites Treccani's Fea entry, which I read and which does not say this. **Uncertain; do not cite Fea.** Vulgar, Goth and market-stall theories are all pre-1651, so "barbarians" is the oldest story, not a modern myth-bust that is unknown.

**What scholars accept now:** every scholarly or academic source I read (Art of Making, Manieri Elia, the-colosseum.net, Wikipedia via Claridge) gives the clamp-robbing explanation for the main pattern of holes on the travertine. None of them mentions market stalls, barbarians or scaffolding as the cause. Hopkins & Beard and Claridge may add nuances I could not read.

## 5. Are all the holes clamp-robbing holes?

| Kind of hole | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Robbing holes at the block joints on the exterior travertine** | **verified** as the main cause | Art of Making PR203_01_07: "The holes visible across this facade were made by the removal of the metal clamps which originally held these blocks in place." Manieri Elia: "That multitude of holes currently visible on all of the travertine facade ... are the traces of the laborious operation ... to retrieve the metal." Holes sit "at the edges" of the blocks per the engineering page I read (engineeringrome.org, an undergraduate blog; weak). |
| **Original lifting holes** in the blocks | **verified (one source)** | Manieri Elia (above): holes "to allow them to be hooked up" in almost all blocks. These are original and need not be at joints. |
| **Putlog / scaffold holes** | **uncertain; no source found** | I searched for putlog or scaffolding holes at the Colosseum and found nothing. Suaresius (p. 9) mentions holes for lifting tongs. Do not say any holes are scaffold holes. |
| **Marble facing / fixture clamps on inner walls** | **verified for at least one passage** | Parco press kit, Commodus Passage, https://colosseo.it/en/press_kit/commodo/ (Italian text): "tracce delle grappe di metallo per il supporto delle lastre" ("traces of the metal clamps that supported the marble slabs"), the walls once being marble-clad, later replaced by painted plaster. Wikipedia (citing Claridge): the interior "was extensively stripped of stone ... or (in the case of the marble facade) was burned to make quicklime". These are a **different** system of clamps from the structural ones. |
| **Velarium mast sockets** | **verified** | 240 mast corbels at the attic (Wikipedia, citing Roth); these are ledges, not the pitted holes. |
| **Pin holes for inscriptions or repairs** | **verified (one source)** | Art of Making PR203_01_14: "a shallow indent for the insertion of an inscription or a repair; two pins holes are visible at either end of this indent." |
| **Modern holes** (boreholes, ties inserted in restorations 1800s and 1978-79) | **verified in general** | Podestà, Romis, Scandolo (2020) describe 19th-century metal ties and the 1978-79 retrofit that used perforations and metal bars. The facade holes in the film should not be assumed Roman or medieval without checking the image. |
| **Bullet holes / bomb damage** | not looked for | Some web pages ask "bullet holes"; I found no scholarly source. |

**Safe wording:** "Most of the holes are where the metal clamps were pulled out." Never "all". Better still: "The big pits you see at the joints are where the metal was dug out."

## 6. How many holes or clamps?

**No source gives a number.** I searched for a count of facade holes or clamps and found none (the 300 tons is a weight, not a count). Do not say "thousands" or "hundreds of thousands". Manieri Elia says "a multitude" (miriade); that is the safest wording ("countless" is also unsourced). A count from the Parco or a photogrammetric survey may exist but I did not find it.

## 8. Surprising, well-sourced items

- **The holes were already a puzzle in the 1600s.** A bishop wrote a whole Latin treatise on them in 1651-52 and began by noting that people explained them as the earthquake at Christ's death ("plainly ridiculous", Suaresius p. 2), as Gothic vandalism, as poles for market-stall awnings, or as barricade posts (section 4). Verified. This is likely unfamiliar and is the best "well-worn myth" angle: the barbarian explanation is 400 years old.
- **The metal was worth more than the wall.** Art of Making: "In the post-Roman period these clamps were more valuable when melted down as raw materials than they were holding these blocks in place." Verified (academic database). Supports "the stone stayed, the metal went".
- **The Church sometimes protected it and sometimes quarried it in the same decade.** Lanciani p. 207: Eugene IV issued a brief to protect the Colosseum from "diggers of marbles" yet used its stones for St John Lateran. Verified. A 1574 decree allowed only stone "in no way attached to any standing part" (p. 210). Both verified.
- Most of the Colosseum's exterior you see today is not the original outer face: Wikipedia (citing Claridge, unread) says "The remainder of the present-day exterior of the Colosseum is in fact the original interior wall." Secondary only; check the shot before saying it.

## 9. Hostile list: five statements an expert commenter will challenge

1. **"300 tons of iron."** Challenge: who counted? It is an unattributed estimate of metal **used**, "iron" is not analysed, and the figure never says "removed" or "stolen". Wording: "Estimates put the metal in the walls at around 300 tons", or "hundreds of tons".
2. **"No mortar."** Challenge: Lugli (as reported by Manieri Elia) says a thin lime slurry was spread between the blocks. Wording: "held by metal clamps, not a mortar bed", or drop it.
3. **"Barbarians" / "Goths" / "in the 6th century" / "in the Middle Ages".** Challenge: no dated evidence for the clamps; the only dated robbing I found (Parco, 11th-13th century) is for the floor. Wording: "In the centuries after Rome fell, people dug the metal out." The Gothic War date is "probably" on one website.
4. **"All the holes are where the clamps were."** Challenge: there are original lifting holes, pin holes for repairs and inscriptions, marble-fixture clamp holes on inner walls, and modern ties. Wording: "Most of the holes".
